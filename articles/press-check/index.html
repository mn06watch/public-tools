Press Check

The Sixth District race is finally getting covered. Here is what the candidates told reporters, checked against the public record, by the same rules for both.

Started September 30, 2026 · Updated October 6, 2026 · Updated as new coverage runs · General election November 3 · Early voting is open
Reader-funded and independent. Help keep this page running through Election Day

The Sixth District race between six-term Rep. Tom Emmer, the House Majority Whip, and DFL challenger Doug Chapin is finally getting local coverage. This page is a companion to it: every checkable claim a candidate makes to a reporter, in the order he makes it, with what the public record shows. We check House votes, federal data, election results, FEC filings, the candidates' own websites and MN-06 Watch's archive of Rep. Emmer's interviews and speeches. Newest coverage is at the top.

How we check, and what the labels mean

Each card shows the claim as the article printed it, marked as the candidate's own words or the reporter's summary (which may quote him in part), and then what the record shows, with links. Both candidates get the same five labels:

Checks out the record supports it Needs context accurate as far as it goes, but the record adds something voters should know Actions don't match the candidate's own votes, statements or conduct cut against what he says Not supported by the record the data says otherwise Not on the record we couldn't find it either way; that is not the same as false

When we mark a claim Not supported by the record, we send it to that candidate's campaign and ask whether they have data or context we missed. If what they send changes the finding, we update the card and note the date. If it doesn't, we add their response to the card.

We check claims, not motives. Where the coverage itself got something wrong, we say so in a separate section. We only see what's published; if we missed something, tell us and we'll correct it with the date noted.

Emmer's recordNot supported by the record

In his words: "[Emmer] has really done nothing for this district in 12 years."

What the record shows

"Nothing" overstates it. Rep. Emmer requested all 15 district projects in this year's spending laws, about $43 million, and he pressed for Stearns County's federal drug-trafficking (HIDTA) designation, granted in 2024.

But he takes credit for more than he did alone. His release says he "secured" all $43 million and doesn't mention that Sens. Klobuchar and Smith also requested six of the projects, $24.7 million including $7 million for Heatherwood Road. Nine, $18.1 million, were his requests alone. And much of the federal road money in the district comes from the 2021 infrastructure law he voted against and called a "socialist wish list"; he has sought and touted funding from programs it expanded, including a 2022 letter asking for a Highway 65 grant.

Sources: Emmer release, Mar. 4, 2026 · Emmer release, Mar. 6, 2024 · Conference earmark table, Transportation-HUD, FY26 (requesters) · Conference earmark table, Commerce-Justice-Science, FY26 · Senate earmark table, Interior, FY26 · Emmer release on Stearns County HIDTA · CNN, Oct. 7, 2022

The districtChecks out

In his words: "Mr. Emmer has won this district with like 60 to 62 percent pretty much the entire time he's been in Congress."

What the record shows

Close. His six wins average 62 percent, and his last two were 62.0% (2022) and 62.4% (2024). The full range: 56.3% (2014), 65.6% (2016), 61.1% (2018), 65.7% (2020).

Sources: MN Secretary of State, 2024 U.S. House results · MN Secretary of State, 2020 · 2014 canvass (Legislative Reference Library)

The districtNeeds context

In his words: "I'm the first candidate really since 2014 who's actually tried to win instead of just putting a name on the ballot."

What the record shows

"Tried to win" is a judgment, but fundraising is one measure. His $438,700 through July 22 is the most any DFL nominee here has raised since at least 2014: Joe Perske $223,413 (2014), David Snyder no FEC reports (2016), Ian Todd $83,119 (2018), Tawnja Zahradka $54,242 (2020), Jeanne Hendricks $205,374 (2022) and $81,016 (2024). Hendricks's 2022 campaign raised close to the 2014 level, so "since 2014" is a stretch.

Sources: FEC: Chapin for Congress · FEC bulk candidate summaries

The districtChecks out

In his words: "33,000 more people voted for Amy Klobuchar than did my predecessor two years ago. Put those 33,000 back in the mix and we're looking at a 53 percent Republican district."

What the record shows

The 33,000 checks out. In the district's 274 precincts in 2024, Klobuchar got 189,223 votes and the DFL House nominee got 155,836, a gap of 33,387. Had those voters backed the DFL House nominee instead of Rep. Emmer, he would have won with 54.4%, in the range Chapin describes.

Sources: MN Secretary of State, 2024 Senate results by precinct · MN Secretary of State, 2024 U.S. House results

The districtChecks out

In his words: "It's the fastest growing district in Minnesota. One in five people has moved here in the last five to ten years."

What the record shows

The district had an estimated 755,084 people in 2024, up 5.9% since the 2020 census, the fastest growth of Minnesota's eight districts. No federal survey measures how many residents moved into the district, so "one in five" is his estimate.

Sources: Census ACS 2024 (Census Reporter)

Emmer's recordChecks out

In his words: "A lot of the work, I-94 expansion, other things across the district are money that was authorized over his vote."

What the record shows

Rep. Emmer voted against the 2021 infrastructure law. Federal records show $330 million of that law's money committed to projects in the district since, including the I-94 expansion project, the Highway 5 project in Victoria and Chanhassen, and the US 169 interchange in Zimmerman.

PricesNeeds context

In his words: "Clearly this war in Iran and the president's trade policies have driven prices up at the grocery store. We're seeing it at the pump."

What the record shows

At the pump, yes: gasoline is up 27.4% from a year ago, driven by the war. Groceries are up a more modest 2.2% so far, and USDA's forecast for 2026 points mostly to bird flu and cattle supply. More may be coming: economists expect the war's higher diesel and fertilizer costs, and tariffs, to work their way into food prices, with one estimate putting the fuel increase alone at 1 to 2 percent on produce.

Sources: BLS CPI, August 2026 · USDA ERS Food Price Outlook · Fortune, Apr. 8, 2026

Boundary WatersNeeds context

In his words: The mining plan is "a Chilean company mining ore to sell to the Chinese."

Twin Metals is owned by Antofagasta, a Chilean company, which sells copper to Chinese smelters elsewhere. Twin Metals hasn't said where Minnesota output would go; the China part is inferred from Antofagasta's past sales, including its supply deals with Chinese smelters.

Boundary WatersChecks out

In his words: "I applaud the governor for halting it in its tracks."

Halted for now. Gov. Walz's Aug. 18 executive order directs state agencies to refuse mining permits and leases in the watershed, and the DNR canceled one Twin Metals state lease in September. It may be temporary: Twin Metals still holds federal rights, a legal challenge is expected, and the Legislature hasn't passed a permanent ban.

TaxesChecks out

In his words: "The 2025 budget law extended tax breaks that overwhelmingly benefit wealthy Americans while increasing federal deficits."

What the record shows

CBO's distributional analysis of the House-passed bill found that, counting the tax cuts together with the Medicaid and SNAP cuts, resources would fall about $1,600 a year for households in the lowest tenth of incomes and rise about $12,000 a year for the highest tenth. CBO estimates the law as signed adds $3.4 trillion to deficits over ten years.

Sources: CBO, June 12, 2025 (distributional analysis) · Roll Call, June 12, 2025 · CBO, Aug. 4, 2025

DebtChecks out

In his words: "The Congressional Budget Office estimates it will increase deficits by trillions of dollars while significantly reducing projected Medicaid spending."

What the record shows

CBO: $3.4 trillion added to deficits over 2025–2034, and about $990 billion less in federal Medicaid and CHIP spending.

Sources: CBO, Aug. 4, 2025 · Georgetown CCF on CBO's coverage estimates

Health careChecks out

In his words: "Tom Emmer voted for the 2025 reconciliation law. The Congressional Budget Office estimates that the law's Medicaid provisions will reduce federal Medicaid spending by hundreds of billions of dollars and increase the number of people without health insurance by 7.5 million in 2034."

What the record shows

Rep. Emmer voted yes on final passage. CBO puts the coverage loss from the Medicaid and CHIP provisions at 7.5 million people in 2034, out of 10 million for the whole law. "Hundreds of billions" undersells CBO's figure: about $990 billion over ten years.

CongressChecks out

In his words: "The House has effectively been frozen at 435 members for nearly a century."

What the record shows

The House reached 435 seats after the 1910 census, and the Permanent Apportionment Act of 1929 fixed it there. Apart from a temporary 437 after Alaska and Hawaii joined, it has stayed at 435.

Sources: U.S. House History, Proportional Representation

SNAPNeeds context

What the record shows

PricesNeeds context

Reporter's summary: "There are things that are still challenging, haven't come down since the Biden administration. Some have ticked up. There are many that have come down." He pointed to eggs.

What the record shows

Eggs are down 23 percent from a year ago. USDA attributes the swing to bird flu: the outbreak that began in 2022 drove egg prices up by killing laying hens, and prices are falling as flocks recover and new outbreaks slow. In February 2025, asked about egg prices, Rep. Emmer said: "This all started because of the excessive spending under the Biden administration."

What has gone up over the past year (BLS, August): gasoline, up 27.4 percent; energy overall, up 16.3 percent; beef, up 5.9 percent; electricity, up 3.8 percent. All prices are up 3.4 percent; food at home, 2.2 percent.

Sources: USDA ERS Food Price Outlook · BLS Consumer Price Index, August 2026 · ABC GMA3, Feb. 10, 2025 (transcript)

PricesChecks out

In his words: "Consumer spending is up 6% over last year."

DebtActions don't match

Reporter's summary: Emphasizes reducing federal debt and spending; the long-term imbalance needs to be addressed.

What the record shows

The largest fiscal vote of his term went the other way. CBO estimates the 2025 reconciliation law he voted for adds $3.4 trillion to deficits over 10 years ($4.1 trillion with interest), and the law raised the debt limit by $5 trillion. What the coverage got wrong or left out

  • Cash on hand. The Emmer profile gives his campaign about $4.5 million on hand as of July 22. The Chapin profile says "almost $5 million." The FEC figure is $4.50 million. FEC
  • Tax-law cost. The Emmer profile's CBO figures ($1.4 trillion to extend 16 provisions, plus $687 billion in interest) come from CBO's June 2025 estimate of the House-passed bill.
