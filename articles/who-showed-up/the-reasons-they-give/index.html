The Reasons They Give

Candidates who skip debates usually say why. Some reasons are about how debates are run, and those have fixes. Others are about not wanting the opponent heard. We sorted them, then checked where each decliner stood in the race when they said no.

When Democrat Jerri Green challenged Sen. Marsha Blackburn to four debates in the Tennessee governor's race, Blackburn said no. She would not, she said, "give air to a socialist candidate."

In Florida, appointed Sen. Ashley Moody turned down a televised debate with Democrat Angie Nixon. She said she wouldn't give Nixon "a stage or any attention."

In Wyoming, Republican Chuck Gray skipped the only televised U.S. House debate. "My job is not to placate my opponents' desire to grandstand on TV," he said.

In Oregon, Sen. Jeff Merkley, a Democrat, declined KTVZ's debate. According to the station, he said he wouldn't debate a candidate he doesn't consider prepared for the job.

Different parties, different states, the same idea: the other candidate doesn't deserve to be heard next to me.

Part 1 of this series found that newsrooms, not campaigns, decide whether voters get the hour when a candidate stays home. This part looks at the candidates: what they say, and where they stood in the race when they said it.

Two kinds of reasons

Read every public explanation in our tracker and the reasons sort into two groups.

About how debates are run. Arizona Gov. Katie Hobbs's campaign said a debate stage is designed for soundbites and theater. Ohio's Amy Acton said she wouldn't amplify what she called baseless lies in her opponent's ads. Florida's David Jolly cited his opponent's "repeated lies and bad faith." Michigan Republican Mike Rogers skipped FOX 2's debate over its choice of moderator, citing media bias.

About not wanting the opponent heard. Blackburn, Moody, Gray and Merkley are above. Alabama's Tommy Tuberville, asked about debating Doug Jones for governor, told a local station there's "no reason to get up there and argue about differences of opinion." His campaign added that there's "nothing new to learn in a debate." Georgia's Rick Jackson said no voter had ever asked him about a debate.

A third group gave no reason at all. Texas Gov. Greg Abbott's campaign ignored debate invitations from Texas news outlets, according to the San Antonio Current. Ken Paxton never accepted any of the Senate debates James Talarico agreed to. This led to one of the funniest replies from Talarico.

In Minnesota's 6th District, Rep. Tom Emmer did not respond to KSTP's interview request or KARE 11's invitation. Indiana Rep. Jefferson Shreve never answered a forum coalition's certified letters.

The best case for saying no

The first group deserves to be taken seriously; the second is an excuse.

A debate is not a neutral venue. It hands each candidate an equal stage and an equal clock, and equal time treats every claim as equally worth hearing. If one candidate is willing to say things that aren't true, the format rewards them: a false claim takes ten seconds to make and two minutes to correct, and the moderator rarely has time to do it. The other candidate can spend the hour rebutting, which means the hour is spent on the opponent's framing.

A candidate who believes that is happening isn't hiding. They are refusing to lend their credibility to a format that, in their view, misleads voters. They can still reach voters through town halls, interviews and ads where they control the frame and can be fact-checked afterward.

That is a principled objection. It's also an objection to a format, which means it has an answer. The solution of not informing the electorate should not be worse than the problem.

Where the objection breaks down

If the problem is unchecked falsehoods, the fix is a moderator who checks them. Acton turned down CNN, which fact-checks in real time. Rogers objected to FOX 2's moderator, then agreed to debates on two other stations, the first held Oct. 8. His objection was to a host, not to debating. Hobbs has now declined general-election debates in two straight campaigns. In 2022 she asked the Clean Elections Commission for separate televised interviews instead; the commission said no. This year her campaign said she would tour the state.

The second group isn't objecting to a format at all. "No reason to argue about differences of opinion" describes exactly what an election is. A policy disagreement is what voters need to see. A label like "socialist" or "grandstanding" is an argument for showing up and rebutting, not for staying home. And whether a challenger is "prepared for the role" is the voters' call to make, after they've seen the candidate questioned.

Running for office is applying for a public job. Debates and forums are part of the interview. The public is doing the hiring. Skipping out on part of the process is akin to hiring someone without completing the job interview.

Ahead, behind, or even

A common suspicion is that candidates who skip debates are simply ahead and see nothing to gain. A UNLV debate coach told ABC News as much: "if you have a very substantial lead, there is very little that you can gain."

We checked. For every candidate in our tracker with a verified decline, we recorded their standing at the time they declined, using race ratings from Sabato's Crystal Ball (as reported on Wikipedia's ratings tables) and nonpartisan public polls where they exist. That's 53 decliners. The answer depends on whether the race is close.

In safe and likely seats (36 decliners), the decliner was favored in 30. Three were trailing; three were unclear. This is the expected pattern: in a district one party almost always wins, the candidate who skips is usually that party's nominee, and there's no competitive pressure to show up. Republicans and Democrats both fit it: Reps. Paul Gosar and Abraham Hamadeh in Arizona, and Democratic Reps. Yassamin Ansari of Arizona and Josh Riley of New York, among others. This is a lesser-discussed cost of safe seats, whether they come from gerrymandering or from where voters live.

In competitive races (17 decliners), it's mixed. Six were favored, six were in races rated even, four were trailing and one was unclear. Paxton and Rogers were slightly behind in their polling averages when they declined. So were Acton in Ohio and Aaron Ford in Nevada. Abbott and Hobbs were favored, narrowly, and Nevada Gov. Joe Lombardo was even or slightly ahead.

So the "they're just ahead" explanation fits safe seats and not much else. In close races, candidates on both sides of the polls are skipping. That cuts against an easy story: the decision to skip isn't only a frontrunner's calculation. It's becoming a habit. And it might be a lack of confidence in one’s own ability to win the argument.

Which is why the obligation can't depend on the polls. A voter in a safe district is owed the same chance to see the candidates questioned as a voter in a toss-up. Every eligible citizen has the right to vote. It shouldn’t matter where they live: every vote counts, and every representative has a duty to present their ideas to their constituents. Voters in safe seats may be owed even more: there, the general election may be the only time the likely winner is asked anything hard before taking office.

What both parties should want

Neither party has a monopoly on this. Our tracker has Republican and Democratic decliners in nearly every region, and in Minnesota alone it has DFL statewide candidates who declined KSTP and a Republican congressman who didn't respond to two stations.

Both parties say they want informed voters. Both parties' voters are the ones left without a side-by-side look when a debate doesn't happen. A party that believes its candidate's positions are better should want them aired next to the opponent's.

If a candidate believes a debate format is broken, the answer is to propose a better one and show up to it. If a candidate believes the opponent shouldn't be heard, that's not a judgment a candidate gets to make for voters.

If you won’t make the case, why are you the best candidate for the job?
