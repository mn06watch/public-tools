Formats That Work

The best argument against debates is that they're theater. This fall, news outlets in at least a dozen states ran something better: long interviews, same-question sit-downs, empty podiums with a moderator who pushes back. Here's what they did.

On Oct. 8, KUNR Public Radio in Reno held a forum for Nevada's governor candidates with an empty podium where Gov. Joe Lombardo would have stood. The next day the station published a short explainer: who was invited, when, what the campaign said, and why the station went ahead. Democrat Aaron Ford answered the questions. Listeners got the hour and the paper trail.

That second step is rare, and it's the model. Part 1 of this series found 30 verified events this fall where a host invited both candidates and gave the time to the one who came. Part 2 sorted the reasons candidates give for staying home and found the strongest of them is about format. This part is about the formats themselves, because if the complaint is that debates are theater, the answer is to run something that isn't.

The case against going ahead

Before the formats, the objection to holding an event with one candidate, at full strength.

A one-candidate event can look like free advertising. The candidate who shows up gets an hour of airtime with no opponent to correct them. The absent candidate's positions get described by a rival, or not at all. A host that does this repeatedly can look like it's taking sides, especially if one party's candidates are the ones skipping. And some hosts can't do it at all: a coalition including League of Women Voters chapters in Indiana and a Syracuse nonprofit outlet said this year that their rules as nonprofits barred one-candidate events.

Better to cancel, the argument goes, than to hand one side an infomercial.

Where it falls short

The objection assumes the event is soft. It doesn't have to be. Every format below puts a prepared journalist in the chair, and the candidate who shows up gets asked hard questions, not handed a microphone. The absent candidate is described by what they said publicly and by the record of the invitation, not by their opponent. And the invitation stays open.

Canceling doesn't make anything fairer. It takes the hour from voters and gives the candidate who stayed home exactly what they wanted.

Six formats running this fall

1. The empty podium, with the published paper trail. The Atlanta Press Club has run its Loudermilk-Young series this way for decades: a candidate who declines is represented by an empty podium, and the debate goes on. On Oct. 12, Democrat Keisha Lance Bottoms used the time to lay out her platform and asked the empty podium the question she would have asked her opponent. KUNR added the piece that makes it credible: a published account of the invitation and the decline. Any station can do this.

2. The long interview in the debate slot. When the DFL candidates for governor, Senate and attorney general declined KSTP's Oct. 2 debate night, the station left their podiums empty and Tom Hauser held extended interviews with the Republicans who came: Lisa Demuth, Michele Tafoya and Ron Schutz. In Oregon, KTVZ turned its Senate debate slot into a town hall with Republican David Brock Smith, with questions from the station's political reporter, after Sen. Jeff Merkley declined. A long interview with follow-ups is often more informative than a debate with 90-second answers.

3. The same questions, separately. Kentucky Educational Television's "Candidate Conversations" sits each candidate down with host Laura Rogers for a half-hour on the same issues. On Oct. 5, Democrat Charles Booker took the Senate slot; KET said on air that Republican Rep. Andy Barr declined. Viewers can compare answers without the crosstalk, and the format holds up whether one candidate shows or both do.

4. Equal-time town halls. After David Jolly declined a debate, Florida A&M's student government held separate town halls for each candidate for governor, with equal time: Byron Donalds on Sept. 29 and Jolly on Oct. 12. Both came. In Ohio, the Columbus Metropolitan Club held a one-hour forum with Vivek Ramaswamy on Oct. 14 and extended the same invitation to Amy Acton. When candidates won't share a stage, this gets both in front of the same audience.

5. A standing public sponsor. Arizona's Citizens Clean Elections Commission runs general-election debates for governor and every congressional district and goes ahead with whoever accepts. This year that meant Rep. Andy Biggs and a No Labels candidate on the governor's stage without Gov. Katie Hobbs, Democrats facing empty spots in four congressional districts where Republican incumbents or nominees didn't take part, and two minor-party candidates facing an empty spot in a fifth, where Democratic Rep. Yassamin Ansari didn't take part. A public sponsor with a fixed schedule removes the scheduling excuse.

6. More than one door. In North Dakota, Rep. Julie Fedorchak declined Prairie Public's televised debate but debated her Democratic opponent on the "Plain Talk" podcast. In upstate New York, a consortium of the Times Union, WAMC, WMHT and other outlets offered one candidate a set date, an alternate and any Monday-through-Thursday night in October. When hosts pool resources and offer several formats, "it doesn't fit my schedule" stops working.

What makes it count

The format matters less than the person asking the questions. What these events have in common:

  • A prepared interviewer. Someone who knows the candidate's record, votes and past statements well enough to notice when an answer doesn't match.
  • Follow-ups. Time to ask the second question, which is where most of the information is, and what gets candidates off their talking points.
  • Fact-checks inside the hour, not the next morning. If a claim is false, say so on air.
  • The invitation on the record. Who was invited, when, what the campaign said. Publish it, like KUNR did.
  • The offer stays open. Say on air that the absent candidate can have the same time, and mean it.

None of this requires a network budget. It requires a decision that the voters' hour isn't contingent on both candidates agreeing to it.

The ask

Broadcasters can do what nonprofits can't: hold the event with one candidate. That puts the responsibility on them.

So the question for every station that has an invitation sitting unanswered: which of these formats are you willing to run, and when?
