Hold the Podium

We checked every House, Senate and governor race on the 2026 ballot. When a candidate refused to debate, some newsrooms gave voters the hour anyway. Others called it off. That choice belongs to the news outlets, not the campaigns.

On the night of Oct. 12, Keisha Lance Bottoms stood next to an empty lectern at Georgia Public Broadcasting. Rick Jackson, her Republican opponent for governor, had declined the Atlanta Press Club's debate. The club went ahead anyway, as its rules require. It was the first time since 1994 that a nominee for Georgia governor had skipped the series.

Four days earlier and about 1,200 miles west, Republican Gregg Hull stood alone at a KOB-TV debate in Albuquerque. Democrat Deb Haaland had withdrawn. The station kept both podiums and held the hour.

Two parties, two empty spots on stage, one choice made by the newsroom both times: the voters still get the hour.

That choice is the subject of this series. MN-06 Watch checked all 506 House, Senate and governor races on the Nov. 3 ballot: whether the major candidates have debated, and every time a host offered airtime to both and only one came. The full race-by-race tracker is here. This is what it shows.

Fewer debates, by choice

Debates used to be the default. In 2018, only two of 36 governor races had no debate between the major candidates, according to an ABC News analysis. In 2022, eight did. This year ABC counted 21 without one scheduled as of late September.

Our count, as of Oct. 16:

  • Governor (36 races): debates held in 19, scheduled in five more, and none coming in at least 10.
  • Senate (35 races): debates held in 15, scheduled in two, and none coming in at least nine.
  • House (435 districts): at least 68 have held or scheduled a debate. For most of the rest we found no record either way, which is not the same as finding there will be none.

The gap isn't one party's habit. Decliners this year include Republicans like Ken Paxton in Texas, Tom Cotton in Arkansas and Rick Jackson in Georgia, and Democrats like Katie Hobbs in Arizona, Amy Acton in Ohio and, in Minnesota, Amy Klobuchar and Peggy Flanagan, who declined KSTP's Oct. 2 debate night. Both have debated or agreed to debate elsewhere.

The case against debates

The strongest argument for skipping a debate isn't about polls. It's about what debates have become.

A modern televised debate gives a candidate 60 to 90 seconds to answer questions about health care, taxes or immigration. The answers that travel afterward are the clips, usually an interruption or an insult, not the policy. When Lauren Boebert and Eileen Laubacher met on 9News on Oct. 12, moderator Kyle Clark had to step in repeatedly as the two talked over each other. That is the format working as designed. We are far from the hallowed days of Lincoln-Douglas debates.

Lies are hard to correct in real time. Amy Acton said she would not debate Vivek Ramaswamy because she didn't want to amplify what she called "baseless lies" in his ads. A false claim made on stage reaches a live audience; the fact-check runs the next day to a smaller one.

Katie Hobbs's campaign put it most directly: face-to-face engagement with voters, it said, works better than "a stage designed for short soundbites and political theater."

Even the people who coach debaters concede the point. Craig Hennigan, who directs the debate team at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas, told ABC News that candidates walk on stage trying not to lose. "You are trying to avoid gaffes," he said. Political scientists add that debates below the presidential level rarely move votes unless someone makes a serious mistake.

If debates are mostly theater, and the theater rewards the candidate who interrupts best, a candidate who skips one is arguably sparing voters a bad product.

That is a real argument. It is an argument for a better debate format and voters asking more of the candidates.

The fix already exists

Every complaint in that case is about format: time limits too short, moderators who don't follow up, falsehoods that go unchecked. None of them is solved by having no event at all, but that’s what we’ve moved closer to. All of them are solved by events news outlets are already running this fall:

  • Kentucky Educational Television runs "Candidate Conversations," long sit-downs where each candidate gets the same questions in the same format.
  • KSTP turned its empty podiums into extended interviews by Tom Hauser, who has covered Minnesota politics for decades and asks follow-ups.
  • Florida A&M students held separate town halls for each governor candidate, with equal time.
  • Arizona's Citizens Clean Elections Commission holds debates for statewide and congressional races and goes ahead with whoever accepts.

Part 3 of this series looks at these formats in detail. The common thread is a prepared interviewer with time to push back. The answer to bad debates isn’t a less informed electorate; it’s making sure the truth gets out.

The decision is the newsroom's

In the races we checked, there were 30 verified events where a host invited both candidates, one declined or didn't respond, and the host gave the time to the one who came. Fourteen were governor or Senate races. In Minnesota, KSTP did it on Oct. 2 for governor and Senate, and both KSTP and KARE 11 aired interviews with Doug Chapin, the DFL candidate in the 6th District, after Rep. Tom Emmer did not appear.

There were also at least 13 events that were called off or never happened after one side said no. Florida A&M canceled its governor debate after David Jolly declined. AARP Virginia and CBS 6 canceled a Senate debate after Sen. Mark Warner couldn't commit. In Indiana's 6th District, a coalition of 13 nonpartisan groups canceled its forum after Rep. Jefferson Shreve's campaign never answered invitations sent by certified mail.

Some of those cancellations weren't optional. The Indiana coalition and a Syracuse nonprofit host said their rules as nonprofits barred a one-candidate event. Broadcasters don't face that limit, which puts more of the load on them.

The Atlanta Press Club has held this line for decades. Its debate chair has called the empty podium the club's "strongest lever" for getting candidates to show up. The club's position is simple: canceling punishes the candidate who agreed to come and the voters who tuned in, and rewards the one who stayed home.

A candidate can decide not to debate. Only the newsroom decides whether voters get nothing.

Why this matters

Running for office is applying for a public job. A debate, a forum, a long interview with follow-ups: each is part of the interview, and the public is the hiring committee.

A candidate who won't appear is making a choice. Voters can weigh it. But they can only weigh it if they see it, and an empty podium on live television says more than a line in a news story about a debate that didn't happen.

Both parties should want their candidates on that stage. Both parties' voters lose when the hour goes dark. And more importantly, the general public loses.

“Whenever the people are well informed, they can be trusted with their own government.”— Thomas Jefferson, 1789

But how can the electorate be informed if no one is asking the questions?

There are 18 days until the election. Many of the races in our tracker still have nothing scheduled, including Minnesota's 6th District.

So, to every station, paper and public broadcaster that sent an invitation and got no answer: if the candidate's podium is empty, why should the voters' hour be?
